I
Fresh Maggots
top

First stop after a hurried breakfast of Sugar Smacks was the "summer house" in the back garden. The summer house, little more than a shed with ambitions to a grander lifestyle, was the headquarters of the two-member Village Anglers Guild. It was also the place where Chris's racing bike, the bait boxes full of old maggots, the garden chairs, the decomposing blue plastic swimming pool with the white painted metal supports, and all his fishing rods were kept. Last night Chris had neatly packed the 7 foot piking rod and the 12 foot livebait rod into his mail-order canvass rod carrier.

Unlike Rit, who kept maggots in the fridge, Chris was content to leave the bait in the summerhouse, which in winter was hardly much warmer than an icebox. Unfortunately, when there was a warm spell the maggots would chrysalise too rapidly, sometimes leaving a solid, caked, wad of casters and only a few slowly moving maggots. Summertime turned the small green plastic bait boxes into a nursery for flies; when the box was opened after a week the newborn flies would spray out like a bursting dandelion. This time the rubbery white grubs were still fresh and active, making their way round the endless circuit of the box.

The grass of the garden was still wet with cold dew, and Chris left footsteps on the lawn as he walked round the side of the garage to the front porch. There he collected his grey tackle box, placed the bait container in the yellow bucket and checked that he had packed his lunch. He preferred peanut butter sandwiches, and today these were supplemented by a Penguin chocolate bar, a banana and a mandarin orange. A small packet of salt and vinegar crisps would be opened later, some to be placed in the sandwiches for extra taste.

He made sure he had lit his hand warmer, an ingenious device that burnt solid fuel inside an asbestos container, neatly packaged in a soft, dark blue pouch. This would be used to keep fingers nimble in the morning session, before they would light the fire. Since all tackle was accounted for, Chris hooked the rod bag over his shoulder, picked up the bucket and bags and marched sleepily down the gravel drive. Next stop was Rit's - this time it was his dad's turn to take them.

Chris pulled open the black iron gates at the bottom of the drive which had been carefully hooked shut by his dad the evening before. He hurried through and let the right gate rebound off the broom bushes at the side of the drive. He didn’t bother to re-shut the gates. In any case, he reasoned, they would be opened soon by his father taking the spotty dog, Gem Bright Star of Orton, for an early morning walk.

Chris walked round the edge of the small village green, a massive chestnut tree at the far end having lost nearly all of its leaves now. The tree was of little interest to the village children most of the time - it was too big to be a goalpost, too tall to be climbed and too strong to be bent. Of course, the autumn revealed its treasure of conkers, although the old tree wasn't considered the place to get the most shiny or biggest chestnuts.

Cutting across the beginning of Mogswell Lane, he walked up Mary Armyne Road, past Mark Stevens' house (who had moved on two years ago but whose house still bore that label to the children), past the Bridges' house and then he turned into the Barnes' house. The old greyish Morris Oxford, that on occasion Chris had seen started with a crank shaft, was in the drive. He left the bucket and rods at the back of the car, squeezed past the side and tried to look in the front room window. He could see Rit moving around in the hall. Turning round the corner of the pre-war house he approached the front door and rang the bell. "Good morning" Rit's dad answered cheerfully as he guided the door ajar. Rit took the opportunity to move onto the doorstep so he could pull up his black wellington boots. They were still covered with mud from the last trip.

Ten minutes later all the gear was packed in the boot of the car, the car was started and carefully reversed out into the road. There was little traffic at this time on a Saturday morning. In fact there was never really anything that could be called traffic in the village; the worst would be a line of three of four vehicles in the morning rush hour. So the drive past the Scout hut, the church, the entrance to the woods (which was also the entrance to Orton Hall School for under-achieving girls, which in those days were cruelly referred to by the children as "mental"), past the doctor's house at the bottom of the village (which Mr Jackson the English teacher was sometimes seen to visit) and finally to the junction with Oundle Road. A left turn took them onto the main road, past the infant, junior and "comprehensive" schools, all of which Chris had attended, then beyond the junction of Orton Waterville.

Five minutes later, after a right turn off Oundle Road, they arrived at the end of the little lane that took them to an intersection of the old Wansford-Peterborough branch line. Mr Barnes stopped next to the house where they had once sheltered from a rainstorm, and they unloaded. The boys said their goodbyes (and thank-yous, they were very polite when it came to guaranteeing a lift for fishing).

They looked down the railway line, and as usual there was nothing to see except a gradual curve in the track as the line made its way to the Orton Staunch crossing. During summer the wooden sleepers would sometimes seep with melted tar, but today they were wet with cold moisture. Quite slippery for boot-clad boys who sometimes had to dash to avoid a friendly punch or man-handle a wet bucket. They ambled up the line. The distance between sleepers was just the right length for their juvenile strides, so they walked in unison on the springy, somewhat decaying wood. As they got older this synchonicity of lengths became less true, when they would take two sleepers at a time in a half-baked goose step.

They reached the point where the line bordered the field leading to the Carp Hole. Now was one of the most critical moments of the day. Two things could go wrong here. Firstly, there could be some adults fishing at the Carp Hole. Secondly, and more terrifying, there could be some fourth year boys, who would be sure to cause problems at the boys' expense. With hearts beating rapidly they ducked under the barbed wire fencing which marked the entrance to the field. Rit lifted up the second layer of wire for Chris and then Chris reciprocated. The barbs would sometimes catch on the rod bags or the shaggy hoods of the "parkas". "No-one there", Chris said triumphantly. With this welcome information they both knew the conditions were as good as they could hope for. There was very little wind, the temperature wasn't too cold, it didn't look like raining and they had the whole of the Carp Hole to themselves.

The Carp Hole was a man-made backwater of the Nene, built by monks who used to farm carp from it. It was a backwater in more than one sense. The boys rarely saw other anglers there and even the boats that were moored on the far side seemed to have no visitors for long periods. The far side was managed by the Peterborough Yacht Club, where berthed three or four boats belonging to members. The largest was directly opposite where Rit and Chris normally fished for livebait. It was also next to the place where it was easiest to squeeze through the Yacht Club fencing, giving them access to the full circumference of the pool of the Carp Hole. The owner of the near side was unknown to the boys; they had never paid for any licence for this spot. Despite the name, Chris and Rit had never caught a carp there but they had been introduced to it as a good piking spot by a friend of Rit. Rit sometimes got to know of better places to go because his brother was five years older. This gave him a channel to the wisdom of the fifth years.

They marched across the field, its wet grass wiping their boots clean as they walked, leaving the black material shiny and slippery. The fields around this area tended to be pasture land for cows, so there was an occasional cow pat to avoid in the gentle undulations of the thickly covered ground. Cow pats were a source of great amusement, either for stepping in or for pushing boys into. When the cows were in the field the boys would pretend to be brave and unconcerned, but they never were able to distinguish a friendly cow from an angry bullock, so sometimes bravery turned to hesitancy.

They arrived at the water’s edge. There was a slight, rising mist over the still water, the smooth grey surface only interrupted by occasional water insects skimming the surface in a false signal of hungry fish. The reeds around the side of the carp hole were tired and stringy, approaching the nadir of their winter plumage. It was ten to nine, the scene looked inviting and there wasn't another angler in sight to share it with.


II
Bleaking
top

The first task was to get the livebait rods in action. This meant they had to extract the rods from the bags, attach the reels, thread the Woolco-brand 4lb breaking strain line through the hand-crafted rings (Chris charged a piecemeal rate for doing the whipping), attach a float, bite on the small lead weights, tie the hook with a special fishing knot, and carefully hook on a lively, white maggot. For good measure Rit threw a handful of maggots into the pool a few metres in front of them. They setup their rod rests, unfolded the canvas and metal chairs (Chris's was blue, Rit's was red), cast out, reeled the line back a few metres and then let the rods sit astride the rests and seats.

Pausing for a few minutes, they took out the handwarmers to give a needed boost to their cold hands. It was still too soon for a fire and in any case the objective now was to concentrate hard on catching bleak, roach or anything else to use as livebait. The Carp Hole didn’t seem to hold gudgeon, so it was usually bleak that they caught. They returned to the long livebait rods, taking them up off the rodrests and settled their feet down into the hard mud which marked the perimeter of the water. In this weather the mud could easily support them, and standing in it gave a lower profile on the horizon - a tactic to avoid scaring the fish.

They re-cast several times, gradually scanning the full angle of the water in front. Rit decided to move round the edge of the Hole, so as to try a new section of water where he had seen a few telltale rings of water. There, on his second cast, he had a bite: "Got one!" he cried, and almost immediately "Bugger, it got off". But it was a hopeful sign, so he changed the chewed, elongated maggot and cast out again. Straightaway his float dipped under, and this time his strike was effective. His rod bent slightly and the line went taught. He reeled rapidly, lifted his rod almost vertical, and swung in a six-inch, shiny silver bleak, the fish spinning as he tried to grab hold of it. The bleak fell off the hook onto the grass and Rit managed to ensure it was in no danger of flipping back into the water.

He skilfully filled the bucket; the only difficulty was getting close enough to the water to fill it up high enough. This achieved, albeit with thick mud riding up his boots, Rit placed the bleak inside, where it swam to the bottom in an apparently happy state unaware of its grisly fate. One livebait ready.

Chris immediately moved over nearer to where Rit was fishing in a not-very-subtle move to cash in on the feeding bleak. Of course, better to have two people catching fish than one that was the argument. Chris cast over to within 6 feet of Rit's float, reeled in slightly and then threw some maggots around his float. A few fish rose to the surface as they snatched at the sinking grubs.

Minutes passed. Neither boy had a bite. The only activity was the occasional ruffle of wind on the grey surface. Then Rit had a vigorous bite, his float bobbing several times before sliding off at an angle. He struck sideways, his rod bent in a tingling arc, and despite his fumbled attempt to re-close the bale arm of his reel, the fish was firmly and fatally hooked. He swung what appeared to be a small roach over to his gloved left hand.

While this was happening Chris's float dipped down and up again before he could react. He reeled in a few inches. The float dipped again, Chris poised to strike - but too late! The float was up again. Nothing else happened for a few minutes, so he reeled in. The maggot was gone, except for a small chewed, sucked, skinny piece of maggot on the barb of the hook. This wouldn't do. Rit had already caught two and was about to start piking. Drastic circumstances called for drastic measures. He would switch to a matchstick - classic bleak-snatching tackle.

A matchstick is so feeble as a float that it cannot even support a small lead weight. So all Chris did was to tie on a used matchstick about two feet above a size 18 gold-coloured hook. The lack of weight also required a different approach to casting. The line was drawn out to a length equivalent to the rod, the reel bale arm was left closed and the tackle was flicked swiftly underarm out into the water. Fortunately the slight breeze was in Chris's favour; the wind dragged the matchstick out further and left it bobbing up and down as it crested the regular ripples of the murky water.

The matchstick dived. Chris jerked his arm back and felt the tell-tale tug of a fish. A long silver bleak shot past him, unhooked itself in mid-air but landed in the grass behind him. It jumped and cavorted in the grass, but was too far from the water-edge for him to worry. Shaking from an adrenalin rush, he set his rod back on a rod rest (one with a black plastic coated U-bend, an extensible rod and a hollow green painted body and a screw to fix its height), stepped up the slight slope and flipped the bleak into the bucket. Inside were now three fish swimming lazily. Rit's first bleak was on its side. Chris tapped on the bucket and the bleak righted itself. It looked like there would be plenty of livebait for piking.

Meanwhile Rit had caught another small roach. Chris panicked slightly when he saw Rit setting up for piking. He knew that the first half-hour of piking was often the most successful period. Rit had a built-cane rod that "weighed a ton". Despite its clumsiness he had caught his biggest pike (a measly 6.25 lbs) with it. He connected the three pieces, threaded the 14lb breaking strain line through the eyes, put on a small red and white plastic bung, tied on a 12" metal trace and a size 6 treble hook. A round lead "bullet" acted as the weight. While he was finishing this off his float shot under, so he rushed back to his livebait rod, struck and reeled in another bleak. For a while he abandoned the piking rod and concentrated on the livebait.


III
Lunch Time
top

It was getting on for 12 o'clock and the boys started to feel a little hungry. They had now caught 13 fish in total. Rit had six and Chris seven. Both had started piking but only half-heartedly; they knew that they would try under the willows and on the other side after lunch and after warming themselves up. A fire was required, not just for heat but also for entertainment. To the left bank of the Carp Hole there was a little sluice regulating a very small outlet from the Carp Hole. They used to cross it to get to Chris's favourite swim, underneath a willow tree, which itself was part of a small woodland. This provided firewood. The two boys shuffled across the narrow sluice, gathering up small twigs and branches, a few dry leaves and moss.

A few trips and they had enough wood for the fire. Rit placed a few twigs and leaves within the frame of a couple of battered, blackened, bricks, which had many months earlier been carried from the railway line. He took the matches from his green parka and coaxed the fire to life. Chris moved his fishing stool to the edge of the fire, took off his gloves and squatted close to the yellow flames, warming his numb hands. Rit likewise positioned himself for fire watching and stoking. Rit prided himself on his fire making skills, learnt in the Cubs and Scouts. He was especially talented in creating a slow-burning, substantial fire, making use of larger branches and small logs.

After a while the fire glowed red, the boys were warm and nimble again. Chris pulled out his white plastic lunch box, opened the crisps, placing them inside the peanut-butter sandwiches. Peanut butter sandwiches tasted much better after they had been "matured" during a morning. Perhaps this was because peanut butter was so bland at fridge temperature. Rit almost always had a round each of egg mayonnaise and lemon curd sandwiches, and they usually had a hint of soap - before making them his dad washed his hands in Imperial Leather.


IV
Livebaiting
top

The fire and food had warmed them up soul and body. Now the serious business was about to begin. The boys decided to try under the Willows first. They reeled in their piking rigs, which they had left hopefully and rather aimlessly about 10 yards out in the water in front of the fire. Rit had caught his first ever pike (one that had swallowed the bait, treble hook included) from that area but lately they hadn't had much success there. When they had first started piking they hooked the bleak through the back - a vicious and pointless tactic that led to the bleak's rapid dying. Wiser although not necessarily kinder, they now hooked livebait through the top lip. However both their livebaits were now exhausted, so they elected to replace them with fresh ones. The tired and ragged bleak, although literally off the hook, were not released - the boys put them back in the bucket as a last resort.

They attached the treble hooks to the bottom ring of their rods. Chris picked up the yellow bucket, Rit carried the rod rests. They manoeuvred back over the sluice, which wasn't that easy with rods. Chris's rod brushed against a wiry shrub, the line tangling on a bushy limb. Not noticing, he kept walking, only to be tugged back, the rod almost dropping from his hand. "Wait a minute" he mumbled. He put the bucket down, slopping water out and giving the fish inside a storm in a pail. He bent and twisted the part of the bush that has snagged his line, eventually freeing his rod, although he left a bouquet of bush stuck on one of the rod rings.

Both boys huddled coldly under the arching Willow branches Their rod rests were fixed deep into the soft mud and the piking tackle was out just in front of them. A freezing drizzle had started, peppering the water and dampening their spirits. The still flickering fire looked inviting on the bank. But the boys were transfixed, they made do with the handwarmers and pulled up the artificial fur-lined hood of the parkas to keep out the cold and rain.

Unhappy with his lie, Rit reeled in, made a side cast to avoid the trees and reeled in again to bring the bung into the pike zone. The still lively bleak caused the bungs to bobble along like feeding birds, moving out from the bank then in, reacting to unseen dangers or currents. Twenty minutes had passed with nothing to disturb their fishy day dreams.

Plop! Rit's bung shot under then came straight up before he could even pick up his rod. Again it dipped. This time it stayed under and the line started sliding off to the left. Rit unlatched his bale arm, allowing the line to be pulled off from the reel. The anglers knew this was a run. Rit's heart thumped against his chest, his fingers trembled and his knees wobbled. Chris too was anticipating: either the excitement of a pike or the relief of a missed run for Rit.

Rit slowly lifted his rod, checked the line was free from the Willow branches and struck hard, low and hopeful to his right. "Got it". The line went taut, the rod strained and arched but it was obvious to both boys that the pike was firmly hooked. Chris looked around, realised their mistake and dashed for the landing net, which they had left by the fire. Rit meanwhile had struggled to bring the pike within one yard of the bank when it gave one last tug, dashing under and away. Rit let the reel turn free, the line pulled out and the reel span round until the pike stopped from exhaustion. It was beaten and could manage only a few tugs as Rit reeled in again. This time Chris was ready to scoop up the pike as it got within two feet of the water's edge. Rit beamed triumphantly and Chris shared in the glory of a competent landing.

They walked in step with rod and full landing net back to the over side of the sluice. They stopped and stooped near the fire. Chris tried to tip the pike out of the net but the treble hook had got stuck in the webbing, leaving the pike dangling from the landing net. The pike flipped on the floor, the side of the mouth still attached to the hook, which became even more tangled in the net. Rit reached for the pink cloth, grabbed the pike, holding it flat on the grassy floor. Chris went to his bag, opened the tackle box (once a Tupperware lunch box) and handed him the silver surgical forceps which they used for gripping the treble hook. Rit used it to twist and yank the hook out of the pike.

The young green and yellow mottled pike was about 20 inches long and looked about 3 lbs (later they weighed it at 2lb 12ozs). This one wasn't worth a photo so Rit slipped it back into the shallow water. It lay stuck on the mud, its back still out of the water. After a few seconds it twisted and slithered into the water, when suddenly it came to its senses, arrogantly flipped its tail and was gone. And despite four more hours of hopeful fishing that was the one and only pike of the day.


V
Darkness and Mist
top

Chris glanced back towards the entrance of the field - his dad was standing waiting in the half-light. It was four o'clock now and the light was fading. A thick, cold and damp mist rolled across the field, making the sighting of the bungs even more difficult. Although they had rarely caught anything at this time of day, they held the unquenchable belief that a few minutes more would produce that "golden" run, the one with the record pike at the other end (or to be more accurate, any pike on the other end). This was one of the times in their lives when they desperately wanted the day to stretch on for hours and hours more. They tried delaying tactics such as packing up all their equipment save the piking rods.

But to no avail, the darkness closed in. The bungs remained stubbornly motionless, even the impaled bleak had given out. The boys admitted defeat and hurriedly dismantled their stuff and started the trudge back across the field.

Meanwhile, Chris's dad, seeing that they had started back, had returned to the brown Fiat Supermirafiori. After five minutes of walking along the line and in heavy darkness the boys packed their stuff haphazardly into the boot. "Right", Mr Firth half-asked, half-affirmed. The trip was over now. The only thing to look forward to was the football results, Dr Who on BBC1, for Chris, the warmed-up roast lunch for tea, and, best of all, the piking trip tomorrow.
